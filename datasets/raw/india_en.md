**India**, officially the **Republic of India**, is a country in [South Asia](South_Asia). It is the [seventh-largest country by area](List_of_countries_and_dependencies_by_area), the [most populous country](List_of_countries_and_dependencies_by_population) in the world and, since its independence in 1947, the world's most populous democracy.[^2] Bounded by the [Indian Ocean](Indian_Ocean) on the south, the [Arabian Sea](Arabian_Sea) on the southwest, and the [Bay of Bengal](Bay_of_Bengal) on the southeast, it shares land borders with [Pakistan](Pakistan) to the west; [China](China), [Nepal](Nepal) and [Bhutan](Bhutan) to the north; [Bangladesh](Bangladesh) and [Myanmar](Myanmar) to the east. In the Indian Ocean, India is near [Sri Lanka](Sri_Lanka) and the [Maldives](Maldives). Its [Andaman and Nicobar Islands](Andaman_and_Nicobar_Islands) share a [maritime border](Maritime_boundary) with Myanmar, [Thailand](Thailand) and [Indonesia](Indonesia).

[Modern humans](Modern_humans) arrived on the [Indian subcontinent](Indian_subcontinent) from [Africa](Africa) no later than 55,000 years ago. Their long occupation, predominantly in isolation as hunter-gatherers, has made the region highly diverse. [Settled life](Neolithic) emerged on the subcontinent in the western margins of the [Indus](Indus_River) [river basin](river_basin) 9,000 years ago, evolving gradually into the [Indus Valley Civilisation](Indus_Valley_Civilisation) of the third millennium BCE. By, an [archaic form](Proto-language) of [Sanskrit](Sanskrit), an [Indo-European language](Indo-European_language), had [diffused](Trans-cultural_diffusion) into India from the northwest. [Its hymns](Rig_Veda) recorded the [early dawnings](Historical_Vedic_religion) of [Hinduism](Hinduism) in India. India's pre-existing [Dravidian languages](Dravidian_languages) were supplanted in the northern regions. By, [caste](caste) had emerged within Hinduism, and [Buddhism](History_of_Buddhism_in_India) and [Jainism](History_of_Jainism) had arisen, proclaiming [social orders](social_order) unlinked to heredity. Early political consolidations gave rise to the loose-knit [Maurya](Maurya_Empire) and [Gupta Empires](Gupta_Empire). During this era, there was a flourishing of creativity in art, architecture, and writing, the status of women declined, and [untouchability](untouchability) became an organised belief. In [South India](South_India), the [Middle kingdoms](Middle_kingdoms_of_India#The_Deccan_plateau_and_South) exported Dravidian language scripts and religious cultures to the kingdoms of [Southeast Asia](Southeast_Asia).

In the [1st millennium](1st_millennium) [Islam](Islam), [Christianity](Christianity), [Judaism](Judaism), and [Zoroastrianism](Zoroastrianism) became established on India's southern and western coasts. Early in the [2nd millennium](2nd_millennium) Muslim armies from [Central Asia](Central_Asia) intermittently overran India's northern plains. The resulting [Delhi Sultanate](Delhi_Sultanate) drew northern India into the cosmopolitan [networks of medieval Islam](Islamic_Golden_Age). In south India, the [Vijayanagara Empire](Vijayanagara_Empire) created a long-lasting composite Hindu culture. In the [Punjab](Punjab), [Sikhism](Sikhism) emerged, rejecting institutionalised religion. The [Mughal Empire](Mughal_Empire) ushered in two centuries of economic expansion and relative peace, and left [a rich architectural legacy](Mughal_architecture). Gradually expanding [rule of the British East India Company](Company_rule_in_India) turned India into a colonial economy but consolidated its [sovereignty](sovereignty). [British Crown rule](British_Raj) began in 1858. The rights promised to Indians were granted slowly, but [technological changes](Industrial_Revolution) were introduced, and modern ideas of education and the public life took root. [A nationalist movement](Indian_independence_movement) emerged in India, the first in the non-European [British Empire](British_Empire) and an influence on other nationalist movements. Noted for nonviolent resistance after 1920,[^23] it became the primary factor in ending British rule.[^24] In 1947, the British Indian Empire was [partitioned](Partition_of_India) into two independent [dominions](Dominion#India_and_Pakistan), a Hindu-majority [dominion of India](dominion_of_India) and a Muslim-majority [dominion of Pakistan](dominion_of_Pakistan). A large-scale loss of life and an unprecedented migration accompanied the partition.

India has been a [federal republic](federal_republic) since 1950, governed through a democratic [parliamentary system](parliamentary_system). It is a [pluralistic](<Pluralism_(political_philosophy)>), [multilingual](Multilingualism) and [multi-ethnic society](Multiculturalism). India's population grew from 361 million in 1951 to over 1.4 billion in 2023. During this time, its nominal [per capita income](per_capita_income) increased from US\$64 annually to US\$2,601, and its literacy rate from 16.6% to 74%. The [Indian economy](Economy_of_India) has since become a [fast-growing](List_of_countries_by_real_GDP_growth_rate) major economy and [a hub for information technology](Information_technology_in_India), with an expanding middle class. India has reduced [its poverty rate](Poverty_in_India), though at the cost of increasing economic inequality. It is a [nuclear-weapon state](List_of_states_with_nuclear_weapons) that [ranks high in military expenditure](List_of_countries_by_military_expenditures). It has disputes over [Kashmir](Kashmir) with its neighbours, Pakistan and China, unresolved since the mid-20th century. Among the socio-economic challenges India faces are [gender inequality](Gender_inequality_in_India), [child malnutrition](Malnutrition_in_India), and rising levels of [air pollution](Air_pollution_in_India). India's land is [megadiverse](megadiverse_country) with four [biodiversity hotspots](biodiversity_hotspots). [India's wildlife](Wildlife_of_India), which has traditionally been viewed with tolerance in [its culture](Culture_of_India), is supported in [protected habitats](Protected_areas_of_India).

## Etymology

According to the _[Oxford English Dictionary](Oxford_English_Dictionary)_, the English proper noun "India" derives most immediately from the [Classical Latin](Classical_Latin) _India_, a reference to a loosely-defined historical region of Asia stretching from [South Asia](South_Asia) to the borders of China. Further [etymons](etymon) are: [Hellenistic Greek](Hellenistic_Greek) (); [Ancient Greek](Ancient_Greek) (), or the River [Indus](Indus); Achaemenian [Old Persian](Old_Persian) (an eastern province of the [Achaemenid Empire](Achaemenid_Empire)); and [Sanskrit](Sanskrit), or "river," but specifically the Indus river, and by extension its well-settled basin. The [Ancient Greeks](Ancient_Greeks) referred to South Asians as, 'the people of the Indus'.

The term _[Bharat](Names_for_India#Bharat)_ (; ), mentioned in both [Indian epic poetry](Indian_epic_poetry) and the [Constitution of India](Constitution_of_India), is used in its variations by [many Indian languages](Names_for_India). A modern rendering of the historical name, which applied originally to [North India](North_India), _Bharat_ gained increased currency from the mid-19th century as a native name for India.

_[Hindustan](Hindustan)_ () is a [Middle Persian](Middle_Persian) name for India that became popular by the 13th century, and was used widely since the era of the [Mughal Empire](Mughal_Empire). The meaning of _Hindustan_ has varied, referring to a region encompassing the northern [Indian subcontinent](Indian_subcontinent) (present-day northern India and [Pakistan](Pakistan)) or to India in its near entirety.

## History

### Ancient India

![Manuscript illustration,, of the Sanskrit epic Ramayana, composed in story-telling fashion ](Battle_at_Lanka,_Ramayana,_Udaipur,_1649-53.jpg) ![Cave 26, a Buddhist shrine, of the rock-cut Ajanta Caves](Cave_26,_Ajanta.jpg)

Based on [coalescence](Coalescent_theory) of [Mitochondrial DNA](Mitochondrial_DNA) and [Y Chromosome](Y_Chromosome) data, it is thought that the earliest extant [lineages](<lineage_(evolution)>) of anatomically modern humans or _[Homo sapiens](Homo_sapiens)_ on the Indian subcontinent had reached there from Africa between 80,000 and 50,000 years ago, and with high likelihood by 55,000 years ago.[^38][^39][^40][^41] Their long occupation, initially in varying forms of isolation as hunter-gatherers, has made the region highly diverse, second only to Africa in human [genetic diversity](genetic_diversity). However, the earliest known modern human fossils in South Asia date to about 30,000 years ago. Evidence for the [neolithic period](neolithic_period) in the western margins of the [Indus](Indus_River) [river basin](river_basin), at [Mehrgarh](Mehrgarh) in [Balochistan, Pakistan](Balochistan,_Pakistan), dates to after. Domestication of grain-producing plants (including [barley](barley)) and animals (including [humped zebu](Zebu) cattle) occurred here. These cultures gradually evolved into the [Indus Valley Civilisation](Indus_Valley_Civilisation), which flourished during in Pakistan and western India. Centred around cities such as [Mohenjo-daro](Mohenjo-daro), [Harappa](Harappa), [Dholavira](Dholavira), [Ganweriwala](Ganweriwala), and [Rakhigarhi](Rakhigarhi), its characteristic features included standardised weights, [steatite](soapstone) [seals](Indus_Valley_Civilisation#Seals), a written script, urban planning, public works, and arts and crafts including pottery styles, terracotta human figures and animal statuettes. Networks of towns and villages grew around the cities in a new [agro](agriculture)-[pastoral](Animal_husbandry) economy.

Between and, an [archaic form](Proto-language) of [Sanskrit](Sanskrit), an [Indo-European language](Indo-European_language), [diffused](Trans-cultural_diffusion) into India from the northwest. Its evidence today is found in the [Rig Veda](Rig_Veda)—the oldest scripture associated with what later became [Hinduism](Hinduism)—which was composed by [Indo-Aryan](Indo-Aryan_languages)-speaking tribes migrating east from what is today northern [Afghanistan](Afghanistan) and across the [Punjab region](Punjab_region).[^44] The settling of the [Ganges](Ganges_river) [river plain](river_plain) took place during the next millennium, when large swathes of the river system's adjoining regions were deforested, at times by setting fires, or later by employing iron implements, and prepared for agriculture. The settlement may have involved driving the preexisting people out or enslaving them. The [Dravidian languages](Dravidian_languages) of India were supplanted in the north, creating a broad [language family](Language_family)-divide, with the Indo-Aryan languages being spoken mainly in the [north](North_India) and [west](West_India), and the Dravidian in some parts of [east India](East_India) and most of the [south](South_India). [Classical Sanskrit](Classical_Sanskrit), a refined and standardised grammatical form would emerge in the mid-1st millennium BCE and was codified in the _[Aṣṭādhyāyī](Aṣṭādhyāyī)_ ('Eight chapters') of [Pāṇini](Pāṇini). The two major Sanskrit epics, the [Mahābhārata](Mahābhārata) and the [Rāmāyaṇa](Rāmāyaṇa), however, were composed in a range of oral storytelling registers called [Epic Sanskrit](Epic_Sanskrit) which was used in northern India between 400 BCE and 300 CE, and roughly contemporary with classical Sanskrit.

A second [urbanisation](urbanisation) had taken place in [South Asia](South_Asia) by, this time on the Ganges plain. In fortified cities, [social differentiation](social_differentiation) by [caste](caste), or [varna](<Varna_(Hinduism)>), had emerged. By the mid-millennium two new ethical and social systems had arisen: [Jainism](History_of_Jainism) based on the teachings of [Mahavira](Mahavira) and [Buddhism](History_of_Buddhism_in_India) on those of [the Buddha](The_Buddha). Both religions stressed non-violence and abjured animal sacrifices conducted in [Brahmanism](Historical_Vedic_religion#Brahmanism), and birth into a fixed hereditary _varna_. By living ethically, lay people could rise socially and morally in these religions. Chronicling the life of the Buddha was central to the beginnings of recorded history in India. The rise of the two religions was a backdrop to the emergence of the first loose-knit geographically extensive power in South Asia, the [Maurya Empire](Maurya_Empire). During the rule of the founder's grandson, [Ashoka](Ashoka) (ca. 268–232 BCE), the empire briefly controlled the major urban hubs and arteries of the subcontinent, except in the deep south. The empire's period was notable for creativity in art, architecture, inscriptions, and produced texts, but also for the declining rights of women in the mainstream [Indo-Aryan speaking](Indo-Aryan_languages) regions. Following his [conquest of Kalinga](Kalinga_War), in which his forces caused immense loss of life, Ashoka adopted [Buddhism](Buddhism), subsequently adopting a policy of _dhamma_ and commissioning numerous [rock and pillar edicts](Edicts_of_Ashoka) throughout the Maurya Empire to promote ethical conduct, non-violence, and social welfare. As the edicts forbade both the killing of wild animals and the destruction of forests, Ashoka is seen by some modern environmental historians as an early embodiment of that ethos.

By the 4th and 5th centuries, the [Gupta Empire](Gupta_Empire) had created a complex system of administration and taxation in the greater Ganges Plain; this system became a model for later Indian kingdoms. Under the Guptas, a renewed Hinduism based on devotion, rather than the management of ritual, began to assert itself. The renewal was reflected in a flowering of art, literature, and science. In South India, the [Sangam literature](Sangam_literature) of the [Tamil language](Tamil_language) reveals that, between and, the southern peninsula was ruled by the [Cheras](Chera_dynasty) and the [Cholas](Chola_dynasty), along the western and eastern plains, respectively, of the [Kaveri river](Kaveri_river) valley, and the [Pandyas](Pandya) farther south along the [Vaigai river](Vaigai_river) valley. By the sixth century, the [Pallavas](Pallava) had grown into a regional power. Simultaneously, Buddhism and Jainism, which had favoured a conservative [transactionalism](transactionalism), were replaced by kingly devotion to the gods of particular places, which became a characteristic of the [Bhakti movement](Bhakti_movement). The Pallavas, in particular, [traded extensively with the Roman Empire](Indo-Roman_trade_relations) and with [West](Western_Asia) and [Southeast Asia](Southeast_Asia).

### Medieval India

![Brihadisvara Temple, built by Chola Rajaraja I between 1003 and 1010 CE](<Brihadisvara_Temple_during_Maha_Shivaratri-WUS03611_(edit).jpg>) ![Calligraphy on the Qutb Minar, built in the Delhi sultanate from 1199 CE to 1220 CE](Close_view_and_art_work_of_Qutb_Minar.jpg) The Indian early medieval age, from, was defined by regional kingdoms and cultural diversity. No ruler of this period was able to create an empire and consistently control lands much beyond their core region. During this time, pastoral peoples, whose land had been cleared to make way for the growing agricultural economy, were accommodated within caste society, as were new non-traditional ruling classes. The caste system consequently began to show regional differences.

In the 6th and 7th centuries, the first [devotional hymns](Bhakti) were composed in Tamil. They were imitated all over India and led to both the resurgence of Hinduism and the development of all [modern languages of the subcontinent](Languages_of_India). Indian royalty, big and small, and the temples they patronised drew citizens in significant numbers to the capital cities, which became economic hubs as well. Temple towns of various sizes began to appear everywhere as India underwent another urbanisation. By the 8th and 9th centuries, the effects were felt in Southeast Asia, as South Indian culture and political systems were exported to lands that became part of modern-day [Myanmar](Myanmar), [Thailand](Thailand), [Laos](Laos), [Brunei](Brunei), [Cambodia](Cambodia), [Vietnam](Vietnam), [Philippines](Philippines), [Malaysia](Malaysia), and [Indonesia](Indonesia). Indian merchants, scholars, and sometimes armies were involved in this transmission; Southeast Asians took the initiative as well, with many sojourning in Indian seminaries and translating Buddhist and Hindu texts into their languages.

After the 10th century, Muslim Central Asian nomadic clans, using [swift-horse](<courser_(horse)>) cavalry and raising vast armies united by ethnicity, repeatedly overran South Asia's north-western plains, leading eventually to the establishment of the Islamic [Delhi Sultanate](Delhi_Sultanate) in 1206. The sultanate was to control much of North India and to make many forays into South India. Although at first disruptive for the Indian elites, the sultanate largely left its vast non-Muslim subject population to its own laws and customs.

By repeatedly repulsing [Mongol raiders](Mongol_Empire) in the 13th century, the sultanate saved India from the devastation visited on West and Central Asia, setting the scene for centuries of [migration](Human_migration) of fleeing soldiers, learned men, mystics, traders, artists, and artisans from that region into the subcontinent, thereby creating a syncretic Indo-Islamic culture in the north. The sultanate's raiding and weakening of the regional kingdoms of South India paved the way for the indigenous [Vijayanagara Empire](Vijayanagara_Empire). Embracing a strong [Shaivite](Shaivism) tradition and building upon the military technology of the sultanate, the empire came to control a large portion of peninsular India, and was to influence South Indian society for long afterwards.

### Early modern India

![The *dargah* of Sufi Salim Chisti, built by Mughal emperor, Akbar, early 17th century](Shaikh_Salim_Chishti_Ka_Maqbara.jpg) ![An East India Company rule gold mohur, issued during the reign of William IIII](India_1835_2_Mohurs.jpg)

In the early 16th century, northern India, then under mainly Muslim rulers, fell again to the superior mobility and firepower of a new generation of Central Asian warriors. The resulting [Mughal Empire](Mughal_Empire) did not stamp out the local societies it came to rule. Instead, it balanced and pacified them through new administrative practices and diverse and inclusive ruling elites, leading to more systematic, centralised, and uniform rule. Eschewing tribal bonds and Islamic identity, especially under [Akbar](Akbar), the Mughals united their far-flung realms through loyalty—expressed through a Persianised culture—to an emperor who had near-divine status.

The Mughal state's economic policies, deriving most revenues from agriculture and mandating that taxes be paid in the well-regulated silver currency, caused peasants and artisans to enter larger markets. The relative peace maintained by the empire during much of the 17th century was a factor in India's economic expansion, resulting in greater patronage of [painting](Mughal_painting), literary forms, textiles, and [architecture](Mughal_architecture). Newly coherent social groups in northern and western India, such as the [Marathas](Maratha_Empire), the [Rajputs](Rajput), and the [Sikhs](Sikh_Empire), gained military and governing ambitions during Mughal rule, which, through collaboration or adversity, gave them both recognition and military experience. Expanding commerce during Mughal rule gave rise to new Indian commercial and political elites along the coasts of southern and eastern India. The incremental fragmentation of imperial authority during the late 17th and 18th centuries facilitated a process of decentralisation, enabling provincial elites to assert localised autonomy and consolidate control over their own affairs.

By the early 18th century, with the lines between commercial enterprise and political sovereignty increasingly blurred, European chartered companies—notably the [English East India Company](East_India_Company)—solidified their presence through fortified coastal outposts. The East India Company's control of the seas, greater resources, and more advanced military training and technology led it to assert its military strength increasingly and caused it to become attractive to a portion of the Indian elite; these factors were crucial in allowing the company to gain control over the [Bengal](Bengal) region by 1765 and sideline the other European companies. Its further access to the riches of Bengal and the subsequent increased strength and size of its army enabled it to annexe or subdue most of India by the 1820s. India was no longer exporting manufactured goods as it long had, but instead supplying the [British Empire](British_Empire) with raw materials. Many historians consider this to be the onset of India's colonial period. By this time, with its economic power severely curtailed by the British parliament and having effectively been made an arm of British administration, the East India Company began more consciously to enter non-economic arenas, including education, social reform, and culture.

### Modern India

![Mahatma Gandhi after a visit to the Presidency Jail, Calcutta, April 1938](Mahatma_Gandhi_leaves_Presidency_Jail_in_Calcutta.jpg) ![The Capitol Complex in Chandigarh, commissioned after India's 1947 partition](Palace_of_Assembly_Chandigarh_2006.jpg) The appointment in 1848 of [Lord Dalhousie](James_Broun-Ramsay,_1st_Marquess_of_Dalhousie) as Governor General of the East India Company set the stage for changes essential to a modern state: the consolidation and demarcation of sovereignty, the surveillance of the population, and the education of citizens. Technological changes—among them, railways, canals, and the telegraph—were introduced not long after their introduction in [Europe](Europe). Disaffection with the company also grew during this time and set off the [Indian Rebellion of 1857](Indian_Rebellion_of_1857). Fed by diverse resentments and perceptions, including invasive British-style social reforms, harsh land taxes, and summary treatment of some wealthy landowners and princes, the rebellion rocked many regions of northern and central India and shook the foundations of Company rule. After the rebellion was suppressed in 1858, the East India Company was disbanded, and the British government began to [directly administer India](British_Raj). Proclaiming a [unitary state](unitary_state) and a gradual but limited British-style parliamentary system, the new rulers also protected princes and landed gentry as a feudal safeguard against future unrest. In the decades following, public life gradually emerged across India, eventually leading to the founding of the [Indian National Congress](Indian_National_Congress) in 1885.

The rush of technology and the commercialisation of agriculture in the second half of the 19th century was marked by economic setbacks, and many small farmers became dependent on the whims of faraway markets. There was an increase in the number of large-scale [famines](Famine_in_India), and, despite the risks of infrastructure development borne by Indian taxpayers, little industrial employment was generated for Indians. However, commercial cropping, especially in the newly canalled Punjab, led to increased food production for internal consumption. The railway network provided critical famine relief, notably reduced the cost of moving goods, and helped nascent Indian-owned industry.

After [World War I](World_War_I), in which more than [1.3 million Indians served](Indian_Army_during_World_War_I), the Gandhian era began. It was marked by [British reforms](Montagu–Chelmsford_Reforms) but also [repressive legislation](Rowlatt_act), by more strident Indian calls for self-rule, and by the beginnings of a [nonviolent](nonviolent) movement of non-co-operation led by [Mahatma Gandhi](Mahatma_Gandhi). During the 1930s, the British enacted slow legislative reform; the Indian National Congress won victories in the resulting elections. The next decade was beset with crises: [Indian participation in World War II](India_in_World_War_II), the Congress's final push for non-co-operation, and an upsurge of [Muslim nationalism](Muslim_nationalism_in_South_Asia). All were capped by the advent of independence in 1947, but tempered by the [partition of India](partition_of_India) into two states: India and Pakistan.

India's constitution was adopted in 1950 and established a secular, democratic republic. Economic liberalisation has created a large urban middle class and transformed India into a [fast growing economy](<List_of_countries_by_GDP_(real)_growth_rate>). However, India has been hamstrung by persistent poverty, both rural and urban; [religious](Religious_violence_in_India)- and [caste-related violence](Caste-related_violence_in_India); [Maoist-inspired Naxalite insurgencies](Naxalite–Maoist_insurgency); and [separatism in Jammu and Kashmir](Insurgency_in_Jammu_and_Kashmir) and [in Northeast India](insurgency_in_Northeast_India). India has unresolved territorial disputes with [China](China–India_relations#1960s) and with [Pakistan](Indo-Pakistani_wars_and_conflicts).

## Geography

India accounts for the bulk of the Indian subcontinent, lying atop the [Indian tectonic plate](Indian_Plate), and a part of the [Indo-Australian Plate](Indo-Australian_Plate). India's defining geologic processes began approximately 70 million years ago, when the Indian Plate, then part of the southern supercontinent [Gondwana](Gondwana), began a north-eastward [drift](Plate_tectonics) caused by [seafloor spreading](seafloor_spreading) to its south-west, and later, south and south-east. Simultaneously, the vast [Tethyan](Tethys_Ocean) [oceanic crust](oceanic_crust), to its northeast, began to [subduct](subduction) under the [Eurasian Plate](Eurasian_Plate). The [Indian continental crust](Indian_plate), however, was obstructed and was sheared horizontally. Its lower crust and mantle slid under, but the upper layer piled up in sheets (or [nappes](nappe)) ahead of the subduction zone. This created the [orogeny](orogeny), or process of mountain building, of the Himalayas. The middle and stiffer layer continued to push into Tibet, causing [crustal thickening](Thrust_tectonics) of the [Tibetan Plateau](Tibetan_Plateau). Immediately south of the emerging Himalayas, plate movement created a vast crescent-shaped [trough](<trough_(geology)>) that rapidly filled with river-borne sediment and now constitutes the [Indo-Gangetic Plain](Indo-Gangetic_Plain). The original Indian plate makes its first appearance above the sediment in the ancient [Aravalli range](Aravalli_range), which extends from the [Delhi Ridge](Delhi_Ridge) in a southwesterly direction. To the west lies the [Thar Desert](Thar_Desert), the eastern spread of which is checked by the Aravallis.[^52][^53][^54]

The remaining Indian Plate survives as [peninsular India](South_India), the oldest and geologically most stable part of India. It extends as far north as the [Satpura](Satpura_Range) and [Vindhya](Vindhya_Range) ranges in central India. These parallel chains run from the Arabian Sea coast in Gujarat in the west to the coal-rich [Chota Nagpur Plateau](Chota_Nagpur_Plateau) in Jharkhand in the east. To the south, the remaining peninsular landmass, the [Deccan Plateau](Deccan_Plateau), is flanked on the west and east by coastal ranges known as the [Western](Western_Ghats) and [Eastern Ghats](Eastern_Ghats); the plateau contains the country's oldest rock formations, some over one billion years old. Constituted in such fashion, India lies to the north of the equator between 6° 44′ and 35° 30′ north latitude and 68° 7′ and 97° 25′ east longitude.

Major Himalayan-origin rivers that substantially flow through India include the [Ganges](Ganges) and the [Brahmaputra](Brahmaputra_River), both of which drain into the [Bay of Bengal](Bay_of_Bengal). Important tributaries of the Ganges include the [Yamuna](Yamuna) and the [Kosi](Kosi_River). The Kosi's extremely low gradient, caused by long-term silt deposition, leads to severe floods and course changes. Major peninsular rivers, whose steeper gradients prevent their waters from flooding, include the [Godavari](Godavari_River), the [Mahanadi](Mahanadi_River), the [Kaveri](Kaveri_River), and the [Krishna](Krishna_River), which also drain into the Bay of Bengal; and the [Narmada](Narmada_River) and the [Tapti](Tapti_River), which drain into the [Arabian Sea](Arabian_Sea).

India's coastline measures in length; of this distance, belong to peninsular India and to the Andaman, Nicobar, and Lakshadweep island chains. According to the Indian naval hydrographic charts, the [mainland coastline](Coastal_India) consists of the following: 43% sandy beaches; 11% rocky shores, including cliffs; and 46% [mudflats](mudflat) or marshy shores. Coastal features include the marshy [Rann of Kutch](Rann_of_Kutch) of western India and the alluvial [Sundarbans](Sundarbans) delta of eastern India; the latter is shared with Bangladesh. India has two [archipelagos](archipelago): the [Lakshadweep](Lakshadweep) [coral atolls](Atoll) off India's south-western coast; and the Andaman and Nicobar Islands, a volcanic chain in the [Andaman Sea](Andaman_Sea).

### Climate

The Indian climate is strongly influenced by the Himalayas and the Thar Desert, both of which drive the economically and culturally pivotal summer and winter [monsoons](monsoon). The Himalayas prevent cold Central Asian [katabatic winds](katabatic_wind) from blowing in, keeping the bulk of the Indian subcontinent warmer than most locations at similar latitudes. The Thar Desert plays a crucial role in attracting the moisture-laden south-west summer monsoon winds that, between June and October, provide the majority of India's rainfall.

Four major climatic groupings predominate in India: [tropical wet](Climate_of_India#Tropical_wet), [tropical dry](Climate_of_India#Arid_and_semi-arid_regions), [subtropical humid](Climate_of_India#Subtropical_humid), and [montane](Climate_of_India#Mountain). Temperatures in India have risen by between 1901 and 2018. [Climate change in India](Climate_change_in_India) is often thought to be the cause. The [retreat of Himalayan glaciers](Retreat_of_glaciers_since_1850) has adversely affected the [flow rate](Volumetric_flow_rate) of the major Himalayan rivers, including the [Ganges](Ganges) and the [Brahmaputra](Brahmaputra). According to some current projections, the number and severity of droughts in India will have markedly increased by the end of the present century. ![Indian coral tree in bloom in the mist of the Southwest Monsoon, Mallalli Falls, Hassan, Karnataka](Coral_Tree_Monsoon_Mallalli_Falls_Hassan_Jun24_A7CR_01575.jpg)

### Biodiversity

India is a [megadiverse country](megadiverse_country), a term employed for 17 countries that host high [biological diversity](biological_diversity) and contain many species [indigenous](<indigenous_(ecology)>), or [endemic](endemic), to them. India is the [habitat](habitat) for 8.6% of all [mammals](mammal), 13.7% of [bird](bird) species, 7.9% of [reptile](reptile) species, 6% of [amphibian](amphibian) species, 12.2% of [fish](fish) species, and 6.0% of all [flowering plant](flowering_plant) species. Fully a third of Indian plant species are endemic. India also overlaps four of the world's 34 [biodiversity hotspots](biodiversity_hotspot), or regions that display significant habitat loss in the presence of high endemism.

India's most dense forests, such as the [tropical moist forest](tropical_and_subtropical_moist_broadleaf_forest) of the [Andaman Islands](Andaman_Islands), the [Western Ghats](Western_Ghats), and [Northeast India](Northeast_India), occupy approximately 3% of its land area. _Moderately dense forest_, whose canopy density is between 40% and 70%, occupies 9.39% of India's land area. It predominates in the [temperate coniferous forest](temperate_coniferous_forest) of the [Himalayas](Himalaya), the moist deciduous _[sal](Shorea_robusta)_ forest of eastern India, and the dry deciduous [teak](teak) forest of central and southern India. India has two natural zones of [thorn forest](deserts_and_xeric_shrublands), one in the [Deccan Plateau](Deccan_Plateau), immediately east of the Western Ghats, and the other in the western part of the Indo-Gangetic plain, now turned into rich agricultural land by irrigation, its features no longer visible. Among the Indian subcontinent's notable indigenous trees are the [astringent](astringent) _[Azadirachta indica](Azadirachta_indica)_, or _neem_, which is widely used in rural Indian [herbal medicine](herbal_medicine),[^67] and the luxuriant _[Ficus religiosa](Ficus_religiosa)_, or _peepul_, which is displayed on the ancient seals of [Mohenjo-daro](Mohenjo-daro),[^69] and under which [the Buddha](Gautama_Buddha) is recorded in the [Pali canon](Pāli_Canon) to have sought enlightenment.

Many Indian species have descended from those of [Gondwana](Gondwana), the southern [supercontinent](supercontinent) from which India separated more than 100 million years ago. India's subsequent collision with Eurasia set off a mass exchange of species. However, [volcanism](Deccan_Traps) and [climatic changes](Climate_variability_and_change) later caused the extinction of many endemic Indian forms. Still later, mammals entered India from Asia through two [zoogeographic](Zoogeography) passes flanking the Himalayas. This lowered endemism among India's mammals, which stands at 12.6%, contrasting with 45.8% among reptiles and 55.8% among amphibians. Among endemics are the vulnerable [hooded leaf monkey](Nilgiri_Langur) and the threatened [Beddome's toad](Duttaphrynus_beddomii) of the Western Ghats.

India contains 172 [IUCN](World_Conservation_Union)-designated [threatened animal species](List_of_endangered_animals_in_India), or 2.9% of endangered forms. These include the endangered [Bengal tiger](Bengal_tiger) and the [Ganges river dolphin](South_Asian_river_dolphin). [Critically endangered](Critically_endangered) species include the [gharial](gharial), a [crocodilian](crocodilian); the [great Indian bustard](great_Indian_bustard); and the [Indian white-rumped vulture](White-rumped_vulture), which has become nearly extinct by having ingested the carrion of [diclofenac](diclofenac)-treated cattle. Before they were extensively used for agriculture and cleared for human settlement, the thorn forests of Punjab were mingled at intervals with open grasslands that were grazed by large herds of [blackbuck](Blackbuck_Conservation_Area) preyed on by the [Asiatic cheetah](Asiatic_cheetah); the blackbuck, no longer extant in Punjab, is now severely endangered in India, and the cheetah is extinct. The pervasive and ecologically devastating human encroachment of recent decades has critically endangered Indian wildlife. In response, the system of [national parks](National_parks_of_India) and [protected areas](protected_areas_of_India), first established in 1935, was expanded substantially. In 1972, India enacted the [Wildlife Protection Act](Wildlife_Protection_Act_of_1972) and [Project Tiger](Project_Tiger) to safeguard crucial wilderness; the Forest Conservation Act was enacted in 1980 and amendments added in 1988. India hosts [more than five hundred wildlife sanctuaries](Wildlife_sanctuaries_of_India) and [eighteenbiosphere reserves](Biosphere_reserves_of_India), four of which are part of the [World Network of Biosphere Reserves](World_Network_of_Biosphere_Reserves); its [eighty-nine wetlands](List_of_Ramsar_sites_in_India) are registered under the [Ramsar Convention](Ramsar_Convention).[^78] ![An *Attacus taprobanis* moth from Kadavoor, Kerala](Attacus_taprobanis-Kadavoor-2018-07-07-001.jpg)

## Government and politics

### Politics

![Narendra Modi has been India's Prime Minister since 2014.](<The_Prime_Minister,_Shri_Narendra_Modi_addressing_the_inaugural_function_of_the_WCIT_India_2018_at_Hyderabad_via_video_conferencing_from_Misuru,_on_February_19,_2018_(1).jpg>) India is a [parliamentary republic](parliamentary_republic) with a [multi-party system](multi-party_system). There are sixrecognised [national parties](List_of_political_parties_in_India#National_parties) in the country, including the [Indian National Congress](Indian_National_Congress) (generally, "the Congress") and the [Bharatiya Janata Party](Bharatiya_Janata_Party) (BJP); there are over 50[regional parties](List_of_political_parties_in_India#State_parties). The Congress is considered the [ideological centre](Centrism) in Indian [political culture](political_culture); the BJP is [right-wing](Right-wing_politics) to [far-right](Far-right_politics).

After India's independence on August 15, 1947, [Jawaharlal Nehru](Jawaharlal_Nehru) became prime minister of the [Dominion of India](Dominion_of_India), an office he held until January 26, 1950, when India became a republic; Nehru remained the caretaker prime minister until the following year. In the general elections in [1951](1951_Indian_general_election), [1957](1957_Indian_general_election), and [1962](1962_Indian_general_election), the Congress, led by Nehru, won by comfortable margins. After Nehru [died](Death_and_state_funeral_of_Jawaharlal_Nehru) in office in May 1964, [Lal Bahadur Shastri](Lal_Bahadur_Shastri) was unanimously chosen by the Congress to be parliamentary leader, and thus prime minister. After the [India–Pakistan war of 1965](India–Pakistan_war_of_1965), Shastri [died](Death_and_state_funeral_of_Lal_Bahadur_Shastri) in January 1966, soon after signing the [Tashkent Peace Declaration](Tashkent_Declaration). The Congress chose [Indira Gandhi](Indira_Gandhi) to be prime minister. She led the party to election victories in [1967](1967_Indian_general_election) and [1971](1971_Indian_general_election), the latter a [landslide](landslide_victory) after Pakistan's defeat in the [Bangladesh Liberation War](Bangladesh_Liberation_War). In 1975, Indira Gandhi declared a [state of emergency](<The_Emergency_(India)>), suspending many civil liberties. Following public discontent with the Emergency, the Congress was voted out of power in [1977](1977_Indian_general_election); [Janata Party](Janata_Party), which had opposed the Emergency, was voted in. Its government lasted two years; [Morarji Desai](Morarji_Desai) and [Charan Singh](Charan_Singh) served as prime ministers. The Congress returned to power in 1980. After a [military operation](Operation_Blue_Star) against [Sikh](Sikhs) militants occupying the [Golden Temple](Golden_Temple) in [Amritsar](Amritsar), Indira Gandhi was [assassinated](Assassination_of_Indira_Gandhi) by a Sikh bodyguard on October 31, 1984. She was succeeded as prime minister by [Rajiv Gandhi](Rajiv_Gandhi), who led Congress to a comfortable victory in the [elections](1984_Indian_general_election) at the end of the year. In 1989, a [National Front](<National_Front_(India)>) coalition, led by the [Janata Dal](Janata_Dal), in alliance with the [Left Front](<Communist_Party_of_India_(Marxist)>), won the [general elections](1989_Indian_general_election). The subsequent government lasted just under two years; [V. P. Singh](V._P._Singh) and [Chandra Shekhar](Chandra_Shekhar) served as prime ministers. In 1991, soon after the first round of polling in the [general election](1991_Indian_general_election), Congress leader Rajiv Gandhi [was assassinated](Assassination_of_Rajiv_Gandhi) by a member of a [Sri Lankan Tamil](Sri_Lankan_Tamils) [separatist organisation](Liberation_Tigers_of_Tamil_Eelam) who was seeking to avenge [Indian intervention in the Sri Lankan civil war](Indian_intervention_in_the_Sri_Lankan_civil_war). After the elections, Congress emerged as the largest single party; a new Congress leader, [P. V. Narasimha Rao](P._V._Narasimha_Rao), formed a [minority government](minority_government) which served a full five-year term.

In 1996, the BJP briefly formed a government after winning the [general election](1996_Indian_general_election). [United Front](<United_Front_(India,_1996)>) coalition governments followed, which relied on external political support, [H. D. Deve Gowda](H._D._Deve_Gowda) and [I. K. Gujral](I._K._Gujral) serving as prime ministers. After the [1998 Indian general election](1998_Indian_general_election), [Atal Bihari Vajpayee](Atal_Bihari_Vajpayee) of the BJP became prime minister; his government was short-lived due to the lack of a continued mandate. Elections were held again in [1999](1999_Indian_general_election). The BJP, now a part of the [National Democratic Alliance](National_Democratic_Alliance) (NDA), formed a coalition government led by Vajpayee, who became the first non-Congress prime minister to complete a five-year term. In the [2004 general election](2004_Indian_general_election), the NDA was defeated. Congress emerged as the largest single party and formed a coalition, the [United Progressive Alliance](United_Progressive_Alliance) (UPA). [Manmohan Singh](Manmohan_Singh), a Sikh, who was proposed by Congress President [Sonia Gandhi](Sonia_Gandhi) to be parliamentary leader, served as prime minister of the UPA government, but with some external support. The UPA returned to power in the [2009 general election](2009_Indian_general_election) with increased numbers, no longer dependent on external support. Singh became the first prime minister to be re-elected after Jawaharlal Nehru in 1962. In the [2014 general election](2014_Indian_general_election), the BJP under [Narendra Modi](Narendra_Modi) became the first political party since 1984 to win an absolute majority. The party won a larger majority in the [2019 general election](2019_Indian_general_election). After losing its majority in the [2024 general election](2024_Indian_general_election), the BJP formed a coalition government with its NDA partners. Modi is the longest-serving non-Congress prime minister. [Democratic backsliding](Democratic_backsliding) was found by the 2026 [V-Dem Democracy Report](V-Dem_Institute#Democracy_Report).

### Government

![The original preamble of the Constitution of India in 1950. In 1976, during the tenure of Indian prime minister Indira Gandhi, the first sentence was changed to "Sovereign Socialist Secular Democratic Republic".](Constitution_of_India.jpg "The original preamble of the Constitution of India in 1950. In 1976, during the tenure of Indian prime minister Indira Gandhi, the first sentence was changed to "Sovereign Socialist Secular Democratic Republic".") The Constitution of India was drafted by the [Constituent Assembly of India](Constituent_Assembly_of_India) with uncommon speed and absence of irregularities between 1946 and 1949. The [Government of India Act 1935](Government_of_India_Act_1935) was used as a model and framework. Long passages from the Act were included. The constitution describes a federal state with a parliamentary system of democracy. The federal structure was conspicuous for the strength of the central government, which exclusively exercised control of defence, foreign affairs, railways, ports, and currency. The President, the constitutional head of state, has reserve powers for taking over the administration of a state. The central legislature has two houses: the _[Lok Sabha](Lok_Sabha)_, whose delegates are directly elected by the people in general elections every five years, and the _[Rajya Sabha](Rajya_Sabha)_, whose members are nominated by the elected representatives in the states. There are also features not to be found in the Act of 1935. The definition of fundamental rights is based on the [Constitution of the United States](Constitution_of_the_United_States), and the constitutional directives, or goals of endeavor, are based on the [Constitution of Ireland](Constitution_of_Ireland). An Indian institution recommended by the constitution is the _[panchayat](Panchayati_raj_in_India)_ or village committees. [Untouchability](Untouchability) is illegal (Article 17) and caste distinctions are derecognized (Articles 15(2) and 16(2)). The promulgation of the Indian constitution transformed India into a republic within the Commonwealth.

The [prime minister of India](Prime_Minister_of_India) is the [head of government](head_of_government) and exercises most [executive power](<executive_(government)>). Appointed by the president, the prime minister is supported by the [party](political_party) or [political alliance](political_alliance) with a majority of seats in the lower house of parliament. The executive of the Indian government consists of the president, the [vice-president](Vice_President_of_India), and the [Union Council of Ministers](Union_Council_of_Ministers)—with the [cabinet](<Cabinet_(government)>) being its executive committee—headed by the prime minister. Any minister holding a portfolio must be a member of one of the houses of parliament. In the Indian parliamentary system, the executive is subordinate to the legislature; the prime minister and their council are directly responsible to the lower house of the parliament. [Civil servants](Civil_Services_of_India) act as permanent executives and all decisions of the executive are implemented by them.

India has a three-tierunitary [independent judiciary](Judicial_independence) comprising the [supreme court](Supreme_Court_of_India), headed by the [Chief Justice of India](Chief_Justice_of_India), 25[high courts](High_courts_of_India), and a large number of trial courts. The supreme court has [original jurisdiction](original_jurisdiction) over cases involving [fundamental rights](Fundamental_rights_in_India) and over disputes between states and the centre and has [appellate jurisdiction](appellate_jurisdiction) over the high courts. It has the power to both strike down union or state laws which contravene the constitution and invalidate any government action it deems unconstitutional.

### Administrative divisions

India is a federal union comprising 28 [states](States_and_union_territories_of_India) and 8 [union territories](Union_territory). All states, as well as the union territories of [Jammu and Kashmir](<Jammu_and_Kashmir_(union_territory)>), [Puducherry](<Puducherry_(union_territory)>) and the [National Capital Territory of Delhi](Delhi), have elected legislatures and governments following the [Westminster system](Westminster_system). The remaining five union territories are directly ruled by the central government through appointed administrators. In 1956, under the [States Reorganisation Act](States_Reorganisation_Act,_1956), states were reorganised on a linguistic basis. There are over a quarter of a million local government bodies at city, town, district, block and village levels.

### Foreign relations

![In the 1950s and 60s, India played a pivotal role in the Non-Aligned Movement. From left to right: Gamal Abdel Nasser of United Arab Republic (now Egypt), Josip Broz Tito of Yugoslavia and Jawaharlal Nehru in Belgrade, September 1961.](Jawaharlal_Nehru,_Nasser_and_Tito_at_the_Conference_of_Non-Aligned_Nations_held_in_Belgrade.jpg) India remained a member of the [Commonwealth of Nations](Commonwealth_of_Nations) after becoming a republic in 1950. It strongly supported decolonisation in Africa and Asia in the 1950s, and played a leading role in the Non-Aligned Movement. After cordial relations with China during the greater part of the 1950s, India and China went to [war in 1962](Sino-Indian_War); India was widely thought to have been decisively defeated.[^89] By 1967, however, India was able to fend off [Chinese excursions into Sikkim](Sino-Indian_War_of_1967).

India has had [uneasy relations](Indo-Pakistani_relations) with its western neighbour, Pakistan. The two went to war in [1947](Indo-Pakistani_War_of_1947), [1965](Indo-Pakistani_War_of_1965), [1971](Indo-Pakistani_War_of_1971), and [1999](Kargil_War). Three wars were fought over the [disputed territory of Kashmir](Kashmir_conflict). After the 1965 war, India began to pursue close military and economic [ties with the Soviet Union](India-Soviet_Union_relations). By the late 1960s, the Soviet Union was India's largest arms supplier. China's [nuclear test of 596](<596_(nuclear_test)>) and threats to intervene in support of Pakistan in the 1965 war caused India to produce nuclear weapons. India conducted its [first nuclear weapons test](Smiling_Buddha) in 1974 and carried out [additional underground testing](Pokhran-II) in 1998. India has signed neither the [Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty](Comprehensive_Nuclear-Test-Ban_Treaty) nor the [Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty](Nuclear_Non-Proliferation_Treaty), considering both to be flawed and discriminatory. India maintains a "[no first use](no_first_use)" nuclear policy and is developing a [nuclear triad](nuclear_triad) capability as a part of its "[Minimum Credible Deterrence](Minimum_Credible_Deterrence)" doctrine.

Since the end of the [Cold War](Cold_War), India has increased its economic, strategic, and military cooperation with the [United States](India–United_States_relations) and the [European Union](India–European_Union_relations). In 2008, a [civilian nuclear agreement](U.S.–India_Civil_Nuclear_Agreement) was signed between India and the United States. Although India possessed nuclear weapons at the time and was not a party to the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty, it received waivers from the [International Atomic Energy Agency](International_Atomic_Energy_Agency) and the [Nuclear Suppliers Group](Nuclear_Suppliers_Group), ending earlier restrictions on India's nuclear technology and commerce; India subsequently signed co-operation agreements involving [civilian nuclear energy](Nuclear_power_in_India) with Russia, France, the [United Kingdom](India–United_Kingdom_relations), and [Canada](Canada–India_relations).

## Economy

The Indian economy has been among the ten [largest economies in the world](List_of_countries_by_largest_historical_GDP) since 2010. As of 2026, Indian gross domestic output (GDP) have placed the nation as the [6th largest economy in the world](<List_of_countries_by_GDP_(nominal)>). Measured by [purchasing power parity](purchasing_power_parity) (PPP), India is the [third-largest](<List_of_countries_by_GDP_(PPP)>) economy. It is one of the [world's fastest-growing economies](List_of_countries_by_real_GDP_growth_rate) with an average annual growth rate exceeding 5% from 1990 to 2010. Demographics strain its economy, with the nation historically maintaining [low GDP per capita](<List_of_countries_by_GDP_(nominal)_per_capita>) both regionally and as measured by globally. The vast majority of Indians fall into the global low-income group based on average daily income.

Until 1991, all Indian governments followed [protectionist](protectionism) policies that were influenced by socialist economics. Widespread [state intervention and regulation](Licence_Raj) largely walled the economy off from the outside world. An acute [balance of payments crisis in 1991](1991_Indian_economic_crisis) forced the nation to [liberalise its economy](Economic_liberalisation_in_India); since then, it has moved increasingly towards a free-market system by emphasising both foreign trade and direct investment inflows. India has been a member of [World Trade Organization](World_Trade_Organization) since 1 January 1995.

The 522-million-worker [Indian labour force](Labour_in_India) is the [world's second largest](List_of_countries_by_labour_force),. The service sector makes up 55.6% of GDP, the industrial sector 26.3% and the agricultural sector 18.1%. India's [foreign exchange remittances](Remittance) of US\$100 billion in 2022, the highest in the world, were contributed to its economy by 32 million Indians working in foreign countries. In 2006, the share of external trade in India's GDP stood at 24%, up from 6% in 1985. In 2008, India's share of world trade was 1.6%; in 2021, it was the [world's ninth-largest importer](List_of_countries_by_imports) and the [sixteenth-largest exporter](List_of_countries_by_exports). Between 2001 and 2011, the contribution of petrochemical and engineering goods to total exports grew from 14% to 42%. India was the [world's second-largest textile exporter](List_of_countries_by_textile_exports) after [China](China) in the 2013 calendar year.

Averaging an economic growth rate of 7.5% for several years before 2007, India has more than doubled its hourly wage rates during the first decade of the 21st century. Some 431 million Indians have left poverty since 1985; India's middle classes are projected to number around 580 million by 2030. In 2024, India's consumer market was the [world's third largest](List_of_largest_consumer_markets). India's nominal [GDP per capita](GDP_per_capita) increased steadily from US\$308 in 1991, when economic liberalisation began, to US\$1,380 in 2010, to an estimated US\$2,731 in 2024. It is expected to grow to US\$3,264 by 2026.

### Industries

The [Indian automotive industry](Automotive_industry_in_India), the world's second-fastest growing, increased domestic sales by 26% during 2009–2010, and exports by 36% during 2008–2009. In 2022, India became the world's third-largest vehicle market after China and the United States, surpassing Japan. At the end of 2011, the [Indian IT industry](Information_technology_in_India) employed 2.8 million professionals, generated revenues close to US\$100 billion equalling 7.5% of Indian GDP, and contributed 26% of India's merchandise exports.

The [pharmaceutical industry in India](pharmaceutical_industry_in_India) includes 3,000 pharmaceutical companies and 10,500 manufacturing units; India is the world's third-largest pharmaceutical producer, largest producer of generic medicines, and supplies up to 50–60% of global vaccines demand, contributing up to 24.44 billion in exports. India's local pharmaceutical market is estimated up to 42 billion. India is among the top 12 biotech destinations in the world.[^105] The Indian biotech industry grew by 15.1% in 2012–2013, increasing its revenues from 204.4 billion ([Indian rupees](Indian_rupee)) to 235.24 billion (US\$3.94 billion at June 2013 exchange rates).

India's capacity to generate electrical power is 300 gigawatts, of which 42 gigawatts is renewable. India emits about 7% of global greenhouse gas emissions. This equates to about 2.5 tons of [carbon dioxide](carbon_dioxide) per person per year, which is half the world average. ## Demographics

With an estimated 1,428,627,663 residents in 2023, India is the world's most populous country. 1,210,193,422 residents were reported in the [2011 provisional census report](2011_Census_of_India). The median age was 28.7 in 2020. Medical advances made in the last 50 years as well as increased agricultural productivity brought about by the "[Green Revolution](Green_Revolution_in_India)" have caused India's population to grow rapidly, though India's decennial rates of growth are decreasing: its population grew by 17.64% from 2001 to 2011, compared to 21.54% growth in the previous decade (1991–2001). The first post-colonial census, conducted in 1951, counted 361 million people. The [life expectancy](life_expectancy) at birth has increased from 49.7 years in 1970–1975 to 72.0 years in 2023. The under-five [mortality](Infant_mortality) rate for the country was 113 per 1,000 live births in 1994 whereas in 2018 it reduced to 41.1 per 1,000 live births.

The human sex ratio, according to the 2011 census, is 940 females per 1,000 males. [Female infanticide in India](Female_infanticide_in_India), and lately [female foeticide](female_foeticide_in_India), have created lop-sided gender ratios; the number of [missing women](missing_women) in the country quadrupled from 15 million to 63 million during the period 1964–2014, faster than the population growth during the same period. According to an Indian government study, an additional 21 million girls are unwanted and do not receive adequate care.[^118] Despite a government ban on sex-selective foeticide, the practice has far from stopped.[^119]

Migration from rural to urban areas has been an important dynamic in India's recent history. The number of people living in urban areas grew by 31.2% between 1991 and 2001. In 2001, over 70% lived in rural areas. The level of urbanisation increased further from 27.81% in the 2001 census to 31.16% in the 2011 census. The slowing down of the overall population growth rate was due to the sharp decline in the growth rate in rural areas since 1991. In the 2011 census, there were 53 [million-plus urban agglomerations in India](List_of_million-plus_urban_agglomerations_in_India). Among them [Mumbai](Mumbai), [Delhi](Delhi), [Kolkata](Kolkata), [Chennai](Chennai), [Bengaluru](Bangalore), [Hyderabad](Hyderabad) and [Ahmedabad](Ahmedabad), in decreasing order by population. ![ The historical development of life expectancy in India from 1881 to 2023](Life-expectancy,_1881_to_2023,_IND.svg)

### Religion

[Religion](Religion) in India is characterised by a diversity of beliefs and practices. Throughout India's history, religion has been an important part of its culture. The [Indian subcontinent](Indian_subcontinent) is the birthplace of four [major world religions](Major_religious_groups): [Buddhism](Buddhism), [Hinduism](Hinduism), [Jainism](Jainism), and [Sikhism](Sikhism). India has the largest population of Hindus, Sikhs, and Jains, the third-largest population of Muslims (after [Indonesia](Indonesia) and [Pakistan](Pakistan)) and the ninth largest of Buddhists. India also has the largest population of people adhering to both [Zoroastrianism](Zoroastrianism) ([Parsis](Parsi) and [Iranis](<Irani_(India)>)) and the [Bahá'í Faith](Baháʼí_Faith_in_India).

The [Preamble to the Constitution of India](Preamble_to_the_Constitution_of_India) declares India to be a [secular state](secular_state), and [freedom of religion](freedom_of_religion) to be a [fundamental right](Fundamental_Rights_in_India) ("... liberty of thought, expression, belief, faith, and worship.") According to the 2011 census of India, 79.8% of the [population of India](demographics_of_India) follows [Hinduism](Hinduism_in_India), 14.2% [Islam](Islam_in_India), 2.3% [Christianity](Christianity_in_India), 1.7% [Sikhism](Sikhism_in_India), 0.7% [Buddhism](Buddhism_in_India) and 0.4% [Jainism](Jainism_in_India). Several tribal religions are also present in India, such as [Donyi-Polo](Donyi-Polo), [Sanamahism](Sanamahism), [Sarnaism](Sarnaism), and [Niamtre](Niamtre).

### Education

The literacy rate in 2011 was 74.04%: 65.46% among females and 82.14% among males. The rural-urban literacy gap, which was 21.2 percentage points in 2001, dropped to 16.1 percentage points in 2011. The improvement in the rural literacy rate is twice that of urban areas. In 2011 [Kerala](Kerala) had the highest literary rate, with 93.91% literacy, and [Bihar](Bihar) the lowest, with 63.82%. In 1981 the respective literacy rates for total population, men and women were 41%, 53% and 29%. In 1951, the rates were 18%, 27% and 9%. In 1921, the rates 7%, 12% and 2%. In 1891, they were 5%, 9% and 1%. According to Latika Chaudhary, in 1911 there were under three primary schools for every ten villages. Statistically, more caste and religious diversity reduced private spending. Primary schools taught literacy, so local diversity limited its growth.

The education system of India is the world's second-largest. India has over 900 universities, 40,000 colleges and 1.5 million schools. In India's higher education system, a significant number of seats are reserved under [affirmative action](Reservation_in_India) policies for the historically disadvantaged. In recent decades India's improved education system is often cited as one of the main contributors to [its economic development](economic_development_in_India). ![Children await school lunch in Rayka (also Raika), a village in Gujarat. ### Health

![Immunisation health workers in 2006. Eight years later, and three years after India's last case of polio, the WHO declared India to be polio-free.1](<Female_health_workers_in_India_(34332433890).jpg>)

India bears a disproportionately large burden of the world's [tuberculosis](Tuberculosis_in_India) rates, with [World Health Organization](World_Health_Organization) (WHO) statistics for 2022 estimating 2.8 million new infections annually, accounting for 26% of the global total. It is estimated that approximately 40% of the population of India carry [tuberculosis](Latent_tuberculosis) infection.

In 2018 [chronic obstructive pulmonary disease](chronic_obstructive_pulmonary_disease) was the leading cause of death after [heart disease](Heart_disease_in_India). The 10 [most polluted cities in the world](List_of_most-polluted_cities_by_particulate_matter_concentration) are all in India with more than 140 million people breathing air 10 times or more over the WHO safe limit. As of 2026, air quality reports show that not a single Indian city meets the WHO's safety guidelines. In cities like Delhi and Mumbai, breathing the air is equivalent to smoking 30 cigarettes a day. In 2017, air pollution killed 1.24 million Indians.

## Culture

### Society

Although sometimes applied to other cultures and religions, caste is a uniquely Indian, and Hindu, social institution. All Hindus fall broadly into four castes, or _[varnas](<Varna_(Hinduism)>)_: [Brahmin](Brahmin), or priests, at the top; below them [Kshatriya](Kshatriya), or warriors; further below, [Vaishya](Vaishya), or merchants and farmers; and at the bottom, [Shudra](Shudra), or the service class. Outside the caste system, and therefore of traditional Hinduism, lie people formerly called "outcastes" or "untouchables," and now [scheduled caste](scheduled_caste) (a term used in India's constitution) or [Dalit](Dalit), a later self-description of pride, meaning "broken" or "downtrodden". Each caste is further divided into sub-castes, or [jatis](Jāti), many of which are tied to occupations. The custom of [endogamy](endogamy), or marrying within one's subcaste, however, makes caste a hereditary label, not of one occupational choice, and has caused the caste system, therefore, to become entrenched. The [Constituent Assembly of India](Constituent_Assembly_of_India) abolished untouchability in 1947, the [Republic of India](Republic_of_India) did more formally in 1950, and India has since enacted other anti-discriminatory laws and social welfare initiatives related to caste. Still, caste-based inequality, discrimination, segregation, and [violence](Caste-related_violence_in_India) persist.

Multi-generational [patrilineal](patrilineal) [joint families](joint_family) have been the norm in India, though [nuclear families](nuclear_family) are becoming common in urban areas. A very large majority of Indians have [their marriages arranged](Arranged_marriage_in_the_Indian_subcontinent) by their parents or family elders. Marriage is thought to be for life; and the divorce rate is extremely low; less than one in a thousand marriages end in divorce. Many women marry before reaching 18, which is their legal marriageable age; [child marriages](child_marriage) are not uncommon, especially in rural areas. In large parts of Hindu northern India, moreover, a form of territorial [exogamy](exogamy) is observed in which a bride marries out of her natal village and her parents do not visit her in her married home; the annual rite [raksha bandhan](raksha_bandhan), during which married women return to their [natal](Birth) homes, has served both to affirm bonds with their natal families and offer a recourse in times of marital stress.[^159] ![A Gond tribe member during the Dandari festival in Jainoor, Telangana. The Gond religion predates the Hindu synthesis of the mid-first-millennium BCE.](Closeup_at_Gussadi_dance_performer.jpg)

### Visual art

India has a very ancient tradition of art, which has exchanged many influences with the rest of [Eurasia](Eurasia), especially in the first millennium. During this period [Buddhist art](Buddhist_art) spread with Indian religions to [Central](Central_Asia), [East](East_Asia) and [Southeast Asia](Southeast_Asia), the last also greatly influenced by Hindu art. Thousands of [seals from the Indus Valley civilisation](Indus_Valley_civilisation#Seals) of the third millennium BCE have been found, usually carved with animals, but also some with human figures. The [Pashupati seal](Pashupati_seal), excavated in [Mohenjo-daro](Mohenjo-daro), Pakistan, in 1928–29, is the best known. Virtually no art survives from a long period following the Indus Valley Civilisation. Almost all surviving ancient Indian art thereafter is in various forms of religious [sculpture](Indian_sculpture) in durable materials, or coins. There was probably originally far more in wood, which is lost. In north India [Mauryan art](Mauryan_art) is the first imperial movement.

Over the following centuries a distinctly Indian style of sculpting the human figure developed, with less interest in articulating precise anatomy than [ancient Greek sculpture](ancient_Greek_sculpture) but showing smoothly flowing forms expressing _prana_ ("breath" or life-force). This is often complicated by the need to give figures multiple arms or heads, or represent different genders on the left and right of figures, as with the [Ardhanarishvara](Ardhanarishvara) form of Shiva and [Parvati](Parvati).

Most of the earliest large sculpture is Buddhist, either excavated from Buddhist [stupas](stupa) such as [Sanchi](Sanchi), [Sarnath](Sarnath) and [Amaravati](Amaravati_Stupa), or is rock cut [reliefs](relief) at sites such as [Ajanta](Ajanta_Caves), [Karla](Karla_Caves) and [Ellora](Ellora). Hindu and Jain sites appear rather later. In spite of this complex mixture of religious traditions, generally, the prevailing artistic style at any time and place has been shared by the major religious groups, and sculptors probably usually served all communities. [Gupta art](Gupta_art), at its peak, is often regarded as a classical period whose influence lingered for many centuries after; it saw a new dominance of Hindu sculpture, as at the [Elephanta Caves](Elephanta_Caves). Across the north, this became rather stiff and formulaic after, though rich with finely carved detail in the surrounds of statues. But in the South, under the [Pallava](Pallava_dynasty) and [Chola dynasties](Chola_dynasty), sculpture in both stone and bronze had a [sustained period of great achievement](Chola_art_and_architecture#Sculpture_and_bronzes); the large bronzes with Shiva as [Nataraja](Nataraja) have become an iconic symbol of India.

Ancient paintings have only survived at a few sites, of which the crowded scenes of court life in the [Ajanta Caves](Ajanta_Caves) are some of the most important. Painted manuscripts of religious texts survive from Eastern India from 10th century onwards, most of the earliest being Buddhist and later Jain. These significantly influenced later artistic styles. The Persian-derived [Deccan painting](Deccan_painting), starting just before the [Mughal miniature](Mughal_miniature), between them give the first large body of secular painting, with an emphasis on portraits, and the recording of princely pleasures and wars. The style spread to Hindu courts, especially [among the Rajputs](Rajput_painting), and developed a variety of styles, with the smaller courts often the most innovative, with figures such as [Nihâl Chand](Nihâl_Chand) and [Nainsukh](Nainsukh). As a market developed among European residents, it was supplied by [Company painting](Company_painting) by Indian artists with considerable Western influence. In the 19th century, cheap [Kalighat paintings](Kalighat_painting) of gods and everyday life, done on paper, were urban [folk art](folk_art) from [Calcutta](Calcutta), which later saw the [Bengal School of Art](Bengal_School_of_Art), reflecting the art colleges founded by the British, the first movement in [modern Indian painting](modern_Indian_painting).

### Mathematics

Significant mathematics began in India in the first millennium BCE. The _[Śulba Sūtras](Shulba_Sutras)_ (literally, "Aphorisms of the Chords" in [Vedic Sanskrit](Vedic_Sanskrit)) (c. 700–400 BCE) contain the earliest extant verbal expression of the [Pythagorean theorem](Pythagorean_theorem) (although very likely it had been known to the [Old Babylonians](First_Babylonian_dynasty).) All mathematical works were orally transmitted until approximately 500 BCE; thereafter, they were transmitted both orally and in manuscript form. The oldest extant mathematical document produced on the Indian subcontinent is the birch bark [Bakhshali manuscript](Bakhshali_manuscript) from the 7th century CE.

In the classical period of Indian mathematics (400 CE to 1200 CE), important contributions were made by [Aryabhata](Aryabhata), [Brahmagupta](Brahmagupta), [Bhaskara II](Bhaskara_II), [Varāhamihira](Varāhamihira), and [Madhava](Madhava_of_Sangamagrama). The [decimal number system](Decimal) in use today[^163] was first recorded in Indian mathematics. Indian mathematicians made early contributions to the study of the concept of [zero](<0_(number)>) as a number,[^165] [negative numbers](negative_numbers),[^166] [arithmetic](arithmetic), and [algebra](algebra). [Trigonometry](Trigonometry)[^168] was further advanced in India, and the modern definitions of [sine](sine) and [cosine](cosine) were developed there.[^169] These mathematical concepts were transmitted to the Middle East, China, and Europe.[^170] A later landmark in Indian mathematics was the development of the [series](<Series_(mathematics)>) expansions for [trigonometric functions](trigonometric_function) (sine, cosine, and [arc tangent](arc_tangent)) by mathematicians of the [Kerala school](Kerala_school_of_astronomy_and_mathematics) in the 15th century CE. Their work, completed two centuries before the invention of [calculus](calculus) in Europe, provided the first example of a [power series](power_series). In the modern era [Srinivasa Ramanujan](Srinivasa_Ramanujan) made fundamental contributions to [number theory](number_theory).

### Music

India contains a wide array of musical practices, including many different [folk musics](Indian_folk_music) from different regions. [Indian classical music](Indian_classical_music) has Vedic origins, and split in the 13th century into the two main traditions of [Hindustani](Hindustani_classical_music) and [Carnatic](Carnatic_music) music. Hindustani is associated with North India and is more [improvisational](Musical_improvisation), featuring instruments such as the [sitar](sitar) and [tabla](tabla), and Carnatic is South Indian and more focused on written [compositions](Musical_composition) such as the _[kriti](<Kriti_(music)>)_, while both styles contain common elements such as the [raga](raga) melodic framework and [tala](<Tala_(music)>) rhythmic meter. Indian music has influenced western genres, including [rock](Rock_music) and [jazz](jazz) musicians during the [1960s counterculture](Counterculture_of_the_1960s).

[Indian film music](Indian_film_music) is music written for [Indian cinema](Cinema_of_India), generally composed by [music directors](List_of_Indian_film_music_directors) and sung by [playback singers](playback_singers). Modern [Indian pop](Indian_pop) takes influences from classical, folk, and western pop music. ![M. S. Subbulakshmi, Carnatic music vocalist](<Sakunthala_1940_filmposter_(2).jpg>)

Classical Dances of India

The best-known classical dance is Bharatnatyam, which began in the temple dances of [Tamil](Tamil_people) [devadasis](devadasi). Identified with "prostitutes and courtesans", their dancing was formally banned in 1947. Concurrently, the dance was rehabilitated as a "pure" art form, with [Rukmini Devi Arundale](Rukmini_Devi_Arundale) as a prominent figure. A devdasi who went on to attain national and international prominence was [Thanjavur Balasaraswati](Balasaraswati). Some sources consider the dance-dramas [Chhau](Chhau_dance) of [Jharkhand](Jharkhand), [West Bengal](West_Bengal), and [Odisha](Odisha) and [Yakshagana](Yakshagana) of [Karnataka](Karnataka) to also belong to the classical tradition.[^182]

Local dance traditions vary widely across India. In addition to the dance-dramas Chhau and Yakshagana, they include dance-dramas [Raslila](Raslila) of western [Uttar Pradesh](Uttar_Pradesh) and [Terukkuttu](Terukkuttu) of Tamil Nadu; calendrical and festival dances such as the [Bhangra](<Bhangra_(dance)>) of Punjab, especially at [Vaisakhi](Vaisakhi), the onset of spring, and [Garba](<Garba_(dance)>) of [Gujarat](Gujarat) during [Navratri](Navratri); and tribal or [Adivasi](Adivasi) dances, such as those of the [Santal](Santal) and [Toda people](Toda_people), the latter, for example, in honour of the god [Ön](Toda_people#Religion) who brought buffalo to earth.

Among 20th-century directions is the [modern dance](modern_dance) of [Uday Shankar](Uday_Shankar) in which classical styles were employed but not adhered to rigidly. Examples are dance-dramas based on the ancient Indian [animal fables](Animal_fable), _[Panchatantra](Panchatantra)_, and Nehru's mid-century meditation on Indian history, _[The Discovery of India](The_Discovery_of_India)_. Dance has been an essential aspect of Indian films from the first [talkies](talkies) of the 1930s. The individual and group dances of [Bollywood](Bollywood), for example, show a broad range of influences, including classical, local, and Western popular dance. Towards the end of the 20th century, innovations in [British South Asian](British_South_Asian) music and dance, such as [Post-Bhangra](<Music_of_the_United_Kingdom_(1990s)#Post-Bhangra>), fed back into dance in India.

### Clothing

![Women in sari at an adult literacy class in Tamil Nadu](India_School.jpg) From ancient times until the advent of the modern, the most widely worn traditional dress in India was [draped](Draped_garment). For women it took the form of a [sari](sari), a single piece of cloth many yards long. The sari was traditionally wrapped around the lower body and the shoulder. In its modern form, it is combined with an underskirt, or Indian [petticoat](Petticoat#Asian_petticoats), and tucked in along the waist band for more secure fastening. It is also commonly worn with an Indian [blouse](blouse), or [choli](choli), which serves as the primary upper-body garment, the sari's end—passing over the shoulder—covering the midriff and obscuring the upper body's contours. For men, a similar but shorter length of cloth, the [dhoti](dhoti), has served as a lower-body garment.

The use of stitched clothes became widespread after Muslim rule was established by the [Delhi sultanate](Delhi_sultanate) () and continued by the [Mughal Empire](Mughal_Empire) (). Among the garments introduced during this time and still commonly worn are: the [shalwars](Shalwar_kameez) and [pyjamas](pyjama), both styles of trousers, and the tunics [kurta](kurta) and [kameez](kameez). Shalwars are atypically wide at the waist but narrow to a cuffed bottom. They are held up by a drawstring, which causes them to become pleated around the waist. When the pants are cut quite narrow, on the [bias](<Grain_(textile)#Bias>), they are called [churidars](churidar). The kameez is a long shirt or tunic. Its side seams left open below the waistline.[^196] The kurta is traditionally collarless and made of cotton or silk; it is worn plain or with embroidered decoration, such as [chikankari](chikankari); and typically falls to either just above or just below the wearer's knees.

In the last 50 years, fashions have changed a great deal in India. Increasingly, in urban northern India, the sari is no longer the apparel of everyday wear, though they remain popular on formal occasions. The traditional shalwar kameez is rarely worn by younger urban women, who favour churidars or jeans. In office settings, ubiquitous air conditioning allows men to wear sports jackets year-round. For weddings and formal occasions, men in the middle and upper classes often wear bandhgala, or short [Nehru jackets](Nehru_jackets), with pants, with the groom and his [groomsmen](groomsmen) sporting [sherwanis](sherwani) and churidars. ![A man in dhoti and woollen shawl in Varanasi](<Water_pump,_Varanasi_(15563170660)_Cropped.jpg>)

### Cuisine

[A tandoor chef in Turkman Gate, Old Delhi, makes khameri roti, a Muslim-influenced style of leavened bread.](Making_Khameeri_Roti_in_Tandoor_in_Turkman_Gate_Old_Delhi.webm) The foundation of a typical Indian meal is a cereal cooked plainly and complemented with savoury dishes. The cooked cereal could be steamed rice; [chapati](chapati), a thin unleavened bread; [idli](idli), a steamed breakfast cake; or [dosa](<Dosa_(food)>), a griddled pancake. The savoury dishes might include [lentils](lentil), [pulses](pulses), vegetables, meat, poultry and fish commonly spiced with [ginger](ginger_root) and [garlic](garlic), but also [coriander](coriander), [cumin](cumin), [turmeric](turmeric), [cinnamon](cinnamon), [cardamom](cardamom) and others. In some instances, the ingredients may be mixed during the cooking process. India has distinctive vegetarian cuisines, each a feature of the geographical and cultural histories of its adherents. About 20% to 39% of India's population consists of vegetarians.[^206] Although meat is eaten widely, the proportional consumption of meat is low.

The most significant import of cooking techniques into India during the last millennium occurred during the [Mughal Empire](Mughal_Empire), spreading into northern India from regions to its northwest, along with dishes such as [pilaf](pilaf). Onions, garlic, almonds, and spices were added to the simple yogurt marinade of Persia. Rice was partially cooked and layered alternately with sauteed meat, the pot sealed tightly, and slow cooked according to another Persian cooking technique, to produce [biryani](biryani), a feature of festive dining in many parts of India.

The diversity of Indian food served worldwide has been partially concealed by the dominance of [Punjabi cuisine](Punjabi_cuisine). The popularity of [tandoori chicken](tandoori_chicken)—cooked in a [tandoor](tandoor) oven, which had traditionally been used for baking bread in the rural Punjab and the Delhi region, especially among Muslims, but which is originally from [Central Asia](Central_Asia)—dates to the 1950s, and was caused in large part by an entrepreneurial response among people from the Punjab who had been displaced by the 1947 partition. ![South Indian vegetarian thali, or platter](South_Indian_Thali_Cropped.jpg)

### Sports

Several traditional indigenous sports such as _[kabaddi](kabaddi)_, _[kho kho](kho_kho)_, _[pehlwani](pehlwani)_ and _[gilli-danda](gilli-danda)_, and also [martial arts](Indian_martial_arts), such as _[Kalarippayattu](Kalarippayattu)_ and _[marma adi](marma_adi)_ remain popular. [Chess](Chess) is commonly held to have [originated in India](History_of_chess#India) as _[chaturaṅga](chaturanga)_; There has been a rise in the number of Indian [grandmasters](<Grandmaster_(chess)>). [Viswanathan Anand](Viswanathan_Anand) became the undisputed [Chess World Champion in 2007](World_Chess_Championship_2007) and held the status until 2013. [Parcheesi](Parcheesi) is derived from _[Pachisi](Pachisi)_ another traditional Indian pastime, which in early modern times was played on a giant marble court by [Mughal emperor](Mughal_Empire) [Akbar](Akbar).

[Cricket](Cricket_in_India) is the most popular sport in India. [India](India_national_cricket_team) is one of the more successful cricket teams, having won two [Cricket World Cups](Cricket_World_Cup), three [T20 World Cups](Men's_T20_World_Cup), and three [Champions Trophies](ICC_Champions_Trophy). [India](India_men's_national_field_hockey_team) has won a record eight [field hockey](field_hockey) gold medals in the [summer Olympics](Field_hockey_at_the_Summer_Olympics). ![The Indian hockey team, captained by Dhyan Chand (standing second from l.), after winning the finals at the 1936 Summer Olympics – their third of six consecutive Olympic golds](Indian-Hockey-Team-Berlin-1936.jpg)
